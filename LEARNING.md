# How to learn with Groundwork

Groundwork is built around the handful of learning techniques with the
strongest evidence behind them. This page says what they are, how the tool
uses each one, and what a good session looks like. It's short on purpose.

## The five techniques that work

| Technique | What it means | Where you'll meet it |
|---|---|---|
| **Retrieval practice** | Pulling an answer out of your memory strengthens it far more than reading it again. | Every exercise; *Check yourself* questions; flashcards; reviews |
| **Spacing** | Coming back to an idea after a gap (days, not minutes) makes it last. Cramming feels better and fades faster. | Flashcards (Anki schedules them); exercise reviews 2, 7 and 30 days later |
| **Worked examples, then fading** | Beginners learn fastest from a fully worked example, then problems with less and less help. Being thrown in at the deep end mostly teaches frustration. | Each README's *Learn* section; concept → "fix the bug" → realistic job; hints from Nudge to Answer |
| **Immediate, specific feedback** | Knowing right away exactly what's wrong, and where, stops mistakes from setting in. | Tests re-run on every save, with got/want messages |
| **Self-explanation** | Saying *why* something works, in your own words, finds the gaps reading never shows. | *Explain it back* after each pass; *Answers* that give the reasoning |

Mixing topics (*interleaving*) helps too: when reviews and flashcards come
back in a mixed order, you practise choosing the right tool, not just using
the one the chapter is about.

What doesn't work as well as it feels: rereading, highlighting, and watching
videos without doing anything afterwards. Videos are in the guides for
motivation and a second explanation; the learning happens when you retrieve
and practise.

## A session (25–45 minutes)

1. **Flashcards first (5 minutes).** Do today's Anki reviews on your phone or
   computer. Answer before you flip, out loud if you can.
2. **A due review, if there is one.** The results screen says *↻ Review due*;
   press `v`. Solve it from memory. Struggling a bit is the point: effortful
   recall is what makes it stick.
3. **Read one section of a guide** (`g`), then press `m` when you finish the
   guide. Answer its *Check yourself* questions before reading the answers.
4. **New exercises.** Read the README (`i`), try each task before reaching
   for a hint, and use hints in order. After you pass, *explain it back*, then
   press `s` to compare with the reference solution.
5. **Stop on a pass**, not halfway through a broken file. Starting the next
   session is easiest when the last one ended with a green tick.

Short, frequent sessions beat long, rare ones: five sessions of 30 minutes
in a week will teach you more than one of four hours.

## When you're stuck

- **5 minutes stuck:** reread the task's **Goal** and the README example,
  then press `h` for a Nudge.
- **15 minutes stuck:** take the next hint. Read the failure message slowly:
  file, line, got, want.
- **Still stuck after the Answer:** type it in yourself (don't paste), make
  it pass, and press `s`. The exercise is marked ◐ and comes back as a review
  in two days, so you'll solve it on your own then.
- **Frustrated:** stop. Walk, drink water, come back tomorrow. Sleep is when
  a lot of learning settles.

## Tips for staying with it

- **Lower the cost of starting.** Leave the watcher open; next time, the
  first thing you see is exactly where you stopped and what to do next.
- **One thing on screen.** Read only the task you're on. The **Goal** line is
  enough to begin; the details are there when you need them.
- **Make progress visible.** Press `l` (or run `groundwork stats`) for your
  streak, this week and a grid of the last 12 weeks. Every save counts as
  practice, so a short, messy session still fills in the day. The ✓ marks,
  finished guides and your Anki streak are evidence too, even on days it
  doesn't feel like it.
- **A missed day is fine.** The streak waits until a day is over before it
  resets, and "days this week" matters more than an unbroken run.
- **Use a timer.** 25 minutes on, 5 off. When the timer goes and you're in
  flow, keep going; when you're stuck, take the break.
- **Away from the keyboard** (phone): flashcards and guides only. Exercises
  wait for the computer.

## The research

- Dunlosky et al. (2013), [Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266). Rates ten techniques; practice testing and distributed practice come out on top.
- Roediger & Karpicke (2006), [Test-Enhanced Learning](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x), and Karpicke & Roediger (2008), [The Critical Importance of Retrieval for Learning](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1152408).
- Cepeda et al. (2006), [Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354). The spacing effect across hundreds of studies.
- Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006), [Why Minimal Guidance During Instruction Does Not Work](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15326985ep4102_1). Worked examples and guidance for beginners.
- Chi et al. (1994), [Eliciting Self-Explanations Improves Understanding](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1803_3).
- Rohrer & Taylor (2007), [The shuffling of mathematics problems improves learning](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11251-007-9015-8). Interleaving.
- Brown, Roediger & McDaniel (2014), *Make It Stick: The Science of Successful Learning*. The same ideas as a readable book.
